Things worth knowing

5 facts that explain the whole story

🧵
Apollo's memory was woven by hand
Core rope memory was literally threaded by textile workers — wire through a ring = 1, bypass = 0. 8 weeks per module. Impossible to update after launch. But completely immune to cosmic ray bit-flips.
⚠️
The AGC crashed 5 times on Apollo 11 — and saved itself
During the final landing, overflow alarms fired 5 times. The OS automatically shed low-priority tasks each time and kept guidance running. Armstrong landed with 17 seconds of fuel left.
👁️
Apollo had no hazard detection
No cameras, no LIDAR, no AI. Astronauts used a scale etched in the window to see where the computer was targeting, then hand-flew to avoid boulders. Armstrong dodged a crater by eye.
📡
Apollo's signals were unencrypted — and the Soviets listened
Deliberate choice: encryption adds weight and failure modes. The USSR intercepted every Apollo transmission. Jodrell Bank did too. In 2022, encryption is legally mandated (AES/FIPS 140).
🏭
Orion's processor is a ruggedized commercial chip
Not the exotic $200K radiation-hardened RAD750 — it's the IBM PowerPC 750FX, the same chip family used in the Apple Power Mac G4, hardened by Honeywell. Good enough for the mission profile, a fraction of the cost.
💡
Apollo's computer used the same power as a lightbulb
55 watts. 32 kg. Today's equivalent Orion VMC uses ~20–30W and weighs far less — but delivers roughly 33,000× more compute per watt. The efficiency gain dwarfs the raw speed gain.

Common misconceptions

What most people get wrong

✗
"Orion's computers vote on every decision, like the Space Shuttle"
False. SLS (the rocket) uses voting. Orion does not — each of its 4 computers acts independently. Smart switchboards (PDUs) use a priority list to decide which computer's output to follow. If Computer 1 goes silent, they fall through to 2, 3, then 4. No vote ever happens.
✗
"Orion uses the radiation-hardened BAE RAD750 processor"
Incorrect. Orion uses the IBM PowerPC 750FX — a commercial chip that Honeywell ruggedizes with better boards, vibration resistance, and radiation screening. The RAD750 (used on Curiosity, MRO) is a completely different, much more expensive part designed from the ground up for radiation hardness.
✗
"Artemis has AI that automatically avoids craters during landing"
Not on Orion — Orion doesn't land on the Moon. Autonomous hazard avoidance belongs to the separate Human Landing System (HLS) landers like SpaceX's Starship HLS. These use cameras + LIDAR + Terrain Relative Navigation to autonomously avoid boulders. Orion just orbits and comes home.
✗
"The AGC processed 85,000 instructions per second"
Only for the simplest single-cycle instructions. Real mixed workloads (including arithmetic) averaged ~40,000–43,000 IPS. Multiply operations ran at just ~21,000 per second. The 85K figure is peak, not typical.

Chapter 1

The Apollo Guidance Computer: making miracles fit in a shoebox

The Apollo Guidance Computer — the AGC — was built in the mid-1960s, when "computers" still filled entire rooms. Getting one small enough to fly on a spacecraft was one of the defining engineering challenges of the 20th century. The final design weighed about 32 kg and consumed just 55 watts — roughly the same as a lightbulb.

The AGC ran at a 2 MHz clock, but its effective throughput was closer to 40,000–43,000 instructions per second for realistic workloads — simple instructions could peak at ~85,000, but arithmetic took multiple cycles. It had about 72 KB of read-only program storage and just 4 KB of working memory.

The program memory was literally woven by hand

The storage wasn't a chip — it was called core rope memory, and it was woven. Skilled workers at Raytheon's Waltham, Massachusetts plant hand-threaded wires through tiny magnetic rings: a wire through a ring meant binary 1, bypassing it meant 0. Each module took about 8 weeks to manufacture.

"The software had to be completely finished and frozen months before the mission. Once the wires were woven, there was no patching, no updates, no hot-fixes. What was woven was what flew."

— How core rope ROM worked at Raytheon's Waltham plant

The upside: core rope memory is inherently radiation-immune. Cosmic rays can flip transistor states in modern chips, but they cannot change the physical weave of a wire through a magnetic ring.

~145,000 lines of assembly code, written on paper

Two separate programs flew: Colossus (Command Module) and Luminary (Lunar Module). Together their assembly listings run to roughly 145,000 lines, written on paper coding pads and keypunched onto IBM cards. The project consumed approximately 1,400 person-years of effort at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory — and effectively gave birth to the term "software engineering."

The operating system that saved Apollo 11

What made the AGC remarkable wasn't just its hardware — it was its priority-driven operating system. The custom "Executive" OS could shed lower-priority tasks automatically when overloaded, keeping guidance and navigation running no matter what.

The 1201 and 1202 alarms — July 20, 1969: During Apollo 11's final descent, the AGC threw five "executive overflow" alarms. A rendezvous radar had been accidentally left on, flooding the computer with interrupts. Rather than crashing, the computer autonomously restarted itself five times, each time dropping the non-critical radar task and preserving the landing guidance. A 26-year-old NASA engineer named Jack Garman recognized the codes and gave the call to continue. Armstrong landed with 17 seconds of fuel to spare.

How did astronauts avoid crashing into rocks?

Their eyes. The AGC had no terrain mapping, no cameras, no automated hazard detection. The landing radar measured altitude and descent rate but couldn't detect a boulder. Instead, the Lunar Module window had a graduated scale called the Landing Point Designator (LPD) etched into it. On Apollo 11, Armstrong spotted a 180-meter crater filled with boulders and manually flew around it — piloting the last few hundred meters essentially by feel.

What about backups?

The AGC had no internal hardware redundancy. The Lunar Module carried an independent Abort Guidance System (AGS) built by TRW, capable of handling an abort and ascent — but not landing. Ground-based IBM System/360 mainframes at Mission Control also tracked navigation in parallel throughout every mission.

Chapter 2

Artemis & Orion: distributed computing for deep space

The Artemis program uses two main vehicles: the Space Launch System (SLS) rocket and the Orion capsule. Their computing architectures are fundamentally different from Apollo's — and from each other.

SLS: three computers, all in agreement

The SLS core stage carries three flight computers that require agreement from at least two of three before executing any command. This 2-of-3 majority voting means a single computer glitch is automatically outvoted. SLS only flies for about 8 minutes, so its flight software is lean: around 50,000 lines of code.

Orion: five computers — and they don't vote

Orion carries four primary Vehicle Management Computers (VMCs) plus one backup, built by Honeywell Aerospace. The common assumption is that they vote, like SLS. They don't.

How Orion's computers actually work: Each VMC independently believes it's in charge and sends commands continuously. The Power and Data Units (PDUs) maintain a priority list and execute commands from Computer 1 first. If Computer 1 goes silent, the PDU automatically falls to Computer 2, then 3, then 4. If all four primary computers fail, a fifth running independently-developed software takes over. This is called priority-based failover — not consensus voting.

Each VMC also contains two processors in a self-checking pair: if the two disagree, that VMC stops issuing commands and resets. So Orion has 4 computers × 2 processors + 1 backup = 9 processors total.

The processor inside Orion

Orion's VMCs use the IBM PowerPC 750FX — a commercial chip that Honeywell ruggedized for space with thicker circuit boards, vibration-resistant fasteners, and radiation screening. It is not the BAE Systems RAD750 (used on Curiosity and MRO), which costs roughly $200,000 per unit. The 750FX trades some radiation hardness for substantially higher performance — sensible for Orion's mission profile.

The network connecting everything

Apollo used point-to-point hardwiring. Orion uses Time-Triggered Gigabit Ethernet (TT-GbE) at 1,000 Mbps with three redundant links. Every packet arrives at an exact, pre-scheduled microsecond — no collisions, no delays. This is what keeps Orion's independent computers synchronized without voting.

Communicating from deep space

Apollo peaked at 51.2 kbps. Artemis adds S-band, Ka-band, and an optical laser system (O2O) that demonstrated 260 Mbps during Artemis II testing — a 5,000-fold increase.

What about hazard avoidance for landing?

Orion doesn't land on the Moon. Automated hazard avoidance belongs to the Human Landing System (HLS) vehicles like SpaceX's Starship HLS, which use Terrain Relative Navigation (TRN) and LIDAR — the job Apollo astronauts did entirely by eye.

Chapter 4

Questions you might be wondering about

Several things converged. The AGC's priority OS shed lower-priority tasks automatically under load, keeping guidance running even when overwhelmed — as it did five times during Apollo 11's landing. Core rope memory was inherently reliable and radiation-immune. The software was exhaustively tested with no way to patch it post-launch. And the astronauts themselves were the backup — the system kept human judgment in the loop at every critical phase.

The RAD750 (BAE Systems) is radiation-hardened by design — built from the ground up for space, formally rated to 1 million rads, used on Curiosity and MRO. Cost: ~$200,000 per chip. Max speed ~200 MHz.

The IBM PowerPC 750FX is a commercial chip (the same family as the original Apple Power Mac G4). It runs up to 900 MHz commercially. Honeywell ruggedizes it with better boards, thermal management, and radiation screening, but it doesn't carry RAD750's formal hardness ratings. NASA chose it because Orion's mission profile — lunar flyby and return — exposes it to far less cumulative radiation than a multi-year deep-space mission. The EDAC memory protection and 9-processor redundancy handle the rest.

Entirely deliberate. 1960s encryption hardware meant weight, complexity, and failure modes — unacceptable on a vehicle where every gram mattered. The Soviets intercepted every Apollo transmission. Britain's Jodrell Bank Observatory did too. NASA's view: mission safety through simplicity far outweighed any security benefit.

Artemis operates under NASA-STD-1006 (2019), which mandates AES encryption for command uplinks. The threat model changed: modern spacecraft connect through networked infrastructure that didn't exist in 1969.

Apollo's software was written on paper, keypunched onto IBM cards, and compiled maybe once a day on a shared mainframe. Every line was hand-crafted assembly. Margaret Hamilton's team at MIT effectively invented software engineering in the process.

Orion's ~2.3 million lines use Model-Based Design: engineers build mathematical models in MATLAB/Simulink, and tools auto-generate the C++ code. The software runs on INTEGRITY-178 tuMP — a real-time OS certified to aviation safety standards, with ARINC 653 partitioning ensuring one software function literally cannot crash another. Over 300,000 mission scenarios were tested for SLS software alone.

The Saturn V had its own: the IBM Launch Vehicle Digital Computer (LVDC), handling everything from liftoff through Trans-Lunar Injection. 26-bit words, ~12,190 IPS, genuine triple modular redundancy (three processing paths voting on every output), 138 watts, and liquid cooling. Once the rocket's engines shut down and Orion separated, the AGC took over. Two very different computers solving two very different problems.

The AGC: ~43,000 IPS at 55 watts ≈ 780 instructions per second per watt. Orion VMCs: ~650 million IPS at ~25 watts ≈ 26 million instructions per watt. That's roughly a 33,000-fold improvement in compute efficiency. Every kilogram launched costs thousands of dollars in fuel — lighter, more powerful computers are a core part of how we afford to go back.
